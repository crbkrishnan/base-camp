The remainder clock

A clock with m positions, 0 to m − 1. Count round it a number of times and you land on the number's remainder. Two numbers that land on the same spot are "the same" as far as division by m is concerned — that is all modular arithmetic means.

Five traps

Read before any test

Giving a 'remainder' that is too big

A remainder on division by 6 is one of 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 — nothing else. If x leaves 5, then 2x 'leaves 10' is not an answer; 10 = 6 + 4, so the remainder is 4. Always finish by wrapping round the clock once more.

Treating 'cycle position 0' as the first term

The last digits of 2ⁿ go 2, 4, 8, 6. For 2¹⁰⁰, 100 ÷ 4 leaves 0 — which means the fourth box, 6, not the first. Remainder 0 is the end of a lap, not the start. Sanity check with a small case: 2⁴ = 16 ends in 6.

Dividing remainders

You can add, subtract and multiply remainders freely. You can't divide. 2x leaves remainder 2 on division by 6 does not force x to leave 1: x = 4 works too (2 × 4 = 8 = 6 + 2). Dividing is only safe when the divisor shares no factor with m.

Leaving a negative remainder as the final answer

Using −1 instead of 10 when dividing by 11 is a brilliant shortcut in the working. But the answer to 'what is the remainder' is between 0 and m − 1. If your working ends at −1 on division by 7, the answer is 6.

Off by one on the calendar

100 days after a Monday is 100 mod 7 = 2 days on, so Wednesday — you count forward from Monday, not from Tuesday. And check for 29 February: 2028 is a leap year, 2027 is not. Most calendar marks are lost to one of these two.
