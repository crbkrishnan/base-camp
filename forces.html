Push, Pull
and What Wins

A force is a push or a pull, and that part is easy. The marks go somewhere else: on writing weight in kilograms, on drawing an arrow with no direction on it, on forgetting friction is in the room at all, and on believing that a thing moving at a steady speed must have something pushing it. Four sentences, four dropped marks, every time.

The deal. You've been landing around 10 out of 15. This page is out of 30, so the honest target is 21 on the first pass and 26 on the second. Every force gets three things written down: what it is called, how big it is in newtons, and which way it points. Miss any one of the three and the mark goes with it.

The tug-of-war rig

One crate, two ropes and a floor that fights back. Set a pull on each side, decide whether the floor is rough, and the rig draws every force as an arrow whose length is its size. The number you actually want is the resultant — what is left once the forces have argued it out — because that, and only that, decides what the crate does next. Rightwards counts as positive throughout.

Five traps

Read before any test

Writing a weight in kilograms

50 kg is a mass. A weight is a force, so it is measured in newtons. "The box weighs 50 kg" is wrong in an exam even though everyone says it at home — the box has a mass of 50 kg and weighs 500 N on Earth. Examiners mark the unit, not the sentiment.

An arrow with no direction, or no label

A force has a size and a direction, so a force arrow needs an arrowhead showing which way it points and a label saying what it is and how big it is: weight 300 N, not a bare line. A line with neither is worth nothing, and a line with only one of the two is usually worth half the marks going.

Forgetting that friction is in the room

If something slides, rolls, or moves through air, friction and air resistance are acting on it whether the question mentions them or not. The commonest incomplete diagram in this topic is a box with one forward arrow and nothing pointing back. Draw the opposing force, even when you have to estimate it.

Thinking that moving needs a resultant force

A lorry at a steady 25 m/s has balanced forces on it, not a forward resultant. A resultant force changes motion — it does not maintain it. If the speed is steady and the direction is straight, the resultant is zero, and saying otherwise costs you the whole question every time it comes up.

Adding opposing forces instead of subtracting

90 N right and 40 N left is 50 N to the right, not 130 N. Forces along a line only add when they point the same way. Before you touch the arithmetic, write down which way each force points — and give the answer a direction as well as a number, because "50 N" on its own is half an answer.
