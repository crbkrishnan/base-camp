Small Number,
Big Consequences

The little raised number is an instruction about how many times to multiply, and almost every mark lost in this topic comes from treating it as an ordinary number instead. Get that one idea straight and the six laws stop being things to memorise — they become things you could work out yourself if you forgot them.

The deal. You've been landing around 10 out of 15. This page is out of 30, so the honest target is 21 on the first pass and 26 on the second. Every law here can be checked in ten seconds by writing the multiplication out in full. If you are ever unsure, expand it — that is not cheating, it is what the laws are shorthand for.

Five traps

Read before any test

Reading 34 as 3 × 4

It is 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 81, not 12. The index counts how many threes, it is not something to multiply the base by. This single misreading costs more marks in this topic than every other error combined, and it usually shows up under time pressure in the middle of a longer question.

Combining powers with different bases

23 × 52 cannot be written as a single power of anything sensible. The laws only work when the base is the same on both sides, because only then are you counting the same kind of factor. If the bases differ, check whether one can be rewritten — 4 is 22, 8 is 23, 9 is 32 — and if not, just work both out.

Using the laws on addition

23 + 23 is not 26. The laws are for multiplying and dividing only. Adding two lots of 8 gives 16, which happens to be 24 — but you get there by working it out, not by touching the indices. When you see a plus sign between two powers, expand them.

Forgetting the number in front

(2y2)3 is 8y6, not 2y6. Everything inside the bracket gets cubed, including the 2. Students who write 2y6 have applied the law to the letter and quietly ignored the coefficient sitting next to it.

Thinking a negative index gives a negative answer

2−3 is 1/8, a small positive number. It is not −8 and not −6. A minus sign in the index means reciprocal — turn it upside down — it says nothing at all about the answer being below zero.
