The field plotter

A bar magnet, and a compass you can drag anywhere around it. The needle is not decoration — it is computed from the field at the exact point where you drop it, so it turns as you move. Watch two things. First, the needle's red end always points away from the north pole and towards the south pole, which is the direction a field line runs. Second, the strength number collapses as you move away: the field near a pole is not slightly stronger, it is many times stronger.

The two-magnet bench

Two bar magnets on a bench. Flip either one end-for-end, slide them together or apart, then let go and see what they do. When two like poles face each other the bench marks the neutral point between them — the one place where the two fields are equal and opposite, so a compass dropped there has nothing to line up with. Underneath is a tray of everyday objects. Four of them are metal. Only two of those four are attracted, which is the single fact this topic is built on.

Five traps

Read before any test

Assuming every metal is magnetic

This is the biggest single loss of marks in the topic. Copper, aluminium, brass, gold, silver and zinc are metals and are not magnetic. Only iron, nickel, cobalt and alloys of them — steel above all — are attracted. If a question hands you a copper coin or an aluminium can, it is checking this and nothing else.

Field lines with no arrowheads, or arrows going the wrong way

A line without an arrow records half of what a field line is for. Outside the magnet the arrows run from north to south — out of the red end, round, into the blue end. Arrows pointing into the north pole is the commonest wrong version, and it loses the mark even when the curves are perfect.

Saying attraction proves it is a magnet

It stuck to the magnet, so it is a magnet is worth nothing. An unmagnetised iron nail is attracted to either pole by induced magnetism. Only repulsion proves a magnet, because nothing but a magnet can be pushed away. Write the word repulsion.

Letting field lines cross

Two lines crossing would mean the field at that point pointed in two directions at once, which is meaningless. Lines never cross, never touch and never stop in mid-air — each one runs from the magnet's surface back to the magnet's surface. Crowded diagrams are where crossings sneak in, so draw four or five lines per side, not twenty.

Mixing up the compass needle's pole with the pole it is near

The red end of a compass needle is a north pole, and it points along the field — so near a magnet's north pole it points away, and near a south pole it points towards. The same confusion at planet scale: the Earth's geographic North Pole is a magnetic south pole. Geographic names and magnetic poles are two different labelling systems, and the exam sets questions in the gap between them.
