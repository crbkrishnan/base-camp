Take any whole number apart far enough and you hit primes — and there is only ever one set of them. That single fact is where HCF comes from, where LCM comes from, and why some numbers are perfect squares and others never will be. This page takes numbers apart, then builds them back up.

Five traps

Read before any test

Calling 1 a prime number

A prime has exactly two factors. 1 has only one factor — itself — so it is not prime. It is not composite either; it simply sits outside both. Writing 12 = 1 × 2 × 2 × 3 as a product of primes loses the mark, because the 1 does not belong there.

Getting HCF and LCM the wrong way round

The Highest Common Factor is a factor, so it is never bigger than either number. The Lowest Common Multiple is a multiple, so it is never smaller than either. If your "HCF of 24 and 60" comes out as 120, you have answered the other question — and that single check catches it every time.

Using the sign rule on addition

"Two negatives make a positive" is a rule for multiplying and dividing only. −3 + −4 is −7, not 7. Owing three rupees and then owing four more does not leave you richer. Say the rule out loud with the words "when you multiply" attached to it, or it will leak into every addition you do.

Halving instead of rooting

√64 is 8, not 32. The square root asks "what number times itself gives 64?", not "what is half of 64?" The two happen to agree at 4, which is exactly why the habit survives. Check by squaring your answer back: 8 × 8 = 64, correct.

Losing the bracket on a squared negative

(−5)² is 25, because the whole of −5 gets squared. But −5² is −25, because the power binds to the 5 and the minus sign stays outside. When you square a negative number, write the bracket. It is the difference between two marks and none.
