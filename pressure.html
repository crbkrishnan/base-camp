Spread It or
Concentrate It

The same push can crush a floorboard or leave no mark at all, and nothing about the push has changed — only the area it is spread over. That single idea runs through drawing pins, snowshoes, dams and submarines. What sinks most people is not the physics but two square centimetres pretending to be two square metres.

The deal. You've been landing around 10 out of 15. This page is out of 30, so the honest target is 21 on the first pass and 26 on the second. Write the formula, convert the area into square metres before you divide, then answer with a unit. Skipping the conversion is the single most expensive habit in this topic.

Five traps

Read before any test

Dividing by an area in square centimetres and calling it pascals

60 N on 20 cm² is not 3 Pa. Convert first: 20 cm² = 20 ÷ 10 000 = 0.002 m², so the pressure is 30 000 Pa. The wrong answer is out by a factor of ten thousand and looks perfectly reasonable, which is what makes it dangerous. 1 m² = 10 000 cm², not 100.

Thinking a bigger force always means a bigger pressure

An elephant weighs a hundred times more than a woman in stiletto heels and puts far less pressure on the ground, because its foot is enormous. Pressure is a ratio. Until you know the area you know nothing about the pressure, however big the force is.

Believing liquid pressure depends on how much liquid there is

Look at p = ρgh: there is no volume in it, no width, no shape. A swimming pool and a thin pipe filled to the same depth press equally hard at the bottom. The extra water in the pool sits beside the point, not above it, so it contributes nothing.

Thinking pressure in a liquid only pushes downwards

It acts equally in all directions — down, sideways and up. That is why water squirts horizontally from a hole in the side of a can, and why an upturned glass pushed under water is pushed back up. Write the phrase "in all directions" out loud in the answer; it is frequently a mark on its own.

No unit, or N/m instead of N/m²

2 400 is not an answer; 2 400 Pa is. Pa and N/m² are both accepted, N/m is not, and if the numbers are large the marker will happily take kPa provided you divided by 1 000 and said so. The unit is usually the last mark in the question and the cheapest one on the paper.
