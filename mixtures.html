One Thing,
or Many?

A chemist's "pure" has nothing to do with orange juice. It means one substance and nothing else — and whether a box of particles holds one substance or several is a question you can settle by looking. This page teaches you to look, then to prove it with a thermometer.

The deal. You've been landing around 10 out of 15. This page is out of 30, so the honest target is 21 on the first pass and 26 on the second. Most of the marks here go to three words used precisely — element, compound, mixture — and to one test: a pure substance melts at one temperature, a mixture melts over a range.

The rule it uses is the only one that matters: one substance is pure; more than one is a mixture — and a substance is a compound if its particles contain more than one kind of atom joined together.

The melting-point test

A solid whose data-book melting point is 80 °C, heated at a steady rate. Start with it pure and the graph has a hard, flat shelf at exactly 80. Now stir in impurity and watch the shelf tilt, sink and smear out into a range. This is the test examiners mean by "how could you check that the sample is pure?"

Five traps

Read before any test

Using "pure" the way a juice carton does

"Pure orange juice" is water, sugars, acids and a hundred flavour compounds — a mixture. In chemistry, pure means one substance only. If a question asks whether a sample is pure and you answer "yes, nothing has been added to it", you have answered a different question. Answer with the melting point.

Calling a compound a mixture because it contains two elements

Water contains hydrogen and oxygen, but you cannot filter the hydrogen out. The atoms are chemically joined in a fixed ratio, so water is one substance — a compound, and pure. A mixture is two or more substances that are not joined to each other. Write "joined" or "bonded" when you mean a compound, and "not chemically joined" when you mean a mixture.

Saying a mixture melts "at a lower temperature"

Half the answer. An impure substance melts at a lower temperature and over a range of temperatures, rather than sharply at one. Both halves are separate marks, and the range is the one students forget. The machine above shows it: the shelf sinks and tilts.

Drawing a mixture as two kinds of particle touching in pairs

If two different circles are drawn joined together, you have drawn a compound. A mixture of two elements is two kinds of particle scattered among each other, each kind on its own. Check every diagram you draw: is anything joined that should not be?

Believing that a compound behaves like a bit of each element

Sodium is a metal that explodes in water; chlorine is a poisonous green gas; sodium chloride is table salt. A compound has its own properties, usually nothing like the elements it is made from. A mixture, by contrast, keeps the properties of everything in it — iron filings mixed with sulfur still stick to a magnet.
