A letter is a number nobody has told you yet. That is the whole idea, and every rule on this page is a rule of arithmetic you have used since you were seven — you are just doing it before you know what the number is. Nothing here is new. It only looks new.

The bracket grid

A bracket is the area of a rectangle. The outside number is one side; the inside is the other side cut into two pieces. Read the picture left to right and you have expanded. Read it right to left and you have factorised. It is one picture and two directions, which is why the two skills are really one skill.

Five traps

Read before any test

Adding terms that are not alike

3x + 2 is not 5x. Three x tiles and two unit tiles cannot be pushed into one pile, because you do not know what x is. Test it: let x = 10. Then 3x + 2 = 32, but 5x = 50. The answer 3x + 2 is the simplified form — some expressions simply stop there.

Multiplying only the first term in the bracket

4(2x − 5) is 8x − 20, not 8x − 5. The 4 is a side of the whole rectangle, so it reaches every piece inside. Draw the two cells if you have to — you cannot leave one of them unmultiplied.

Losing the sign when a bracket is subtracted

−2(x − 5) is −2x + 10, not −2x − 10. The minus belongs to the 2, so it multiplies both terms — and minus times minus is plus. This one costs more marks in this topic than everything else put together. Write the −2 into the bracket before you multiply anything.

Confusing 3x² with (3x)²

3x² means "square the x, then multiply by 3". (3x)² means "multiply first, then square the lot", giving 9x². At x = 2 they are 12 and 36. The power only ever binds to what it is touching, unless a bracket says otherwise.

Factorising, but not completely

12x + 18 = 2(6x + 9) is true but unfinished — 6 and 9 still share a 3. The question almost always says completely, and that means taking out the highest common factor: 6(2x + 3). Check by looking inside the bracket: if the two numbers still share a factor, you stopped early.

The four test papers
