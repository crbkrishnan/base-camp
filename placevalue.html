The Point
Never Moves

Multiplying by 0.1 makes a number smaller. Dividing by it makes one bigger. Both feel wrong, and both are obvious the moment you stop imagining the decimal point sliding about and watch the digits move instead. Then rounding stops being a rule and becomes a question about which side of the middle you landed on.

The deal. You've been landing around 10 out of 15. This page is out of 30, so the honest target is 21 on the first pass and 26 on the second. The last two lessons are the ones that pay you back everywhere else: a student who estimates before calculating catches his own mistakes in every other topic on this site.

The place-value board

The decimal point is painted on. It does not move, ever. What moves is the digits — one column left when you multiply by 10, one column right when you multiply by 0.1. Press the buttons and watch which way they slide, and the rule you keep getting backwards will stop being a rule you have to remember.

Five traps

Read before any test

Believing the decimal point moves

It doesn't. The point marks the boundary between whole numbers and parts, and that boundary is fixed. When you multiply by 10, every digit shifts one column to the left. Teaching yourself "move the point right" works until you meet a number like 0.07 × 1000 and cannot tell how many zeros to invent. Move the digits, and the zeros appear on their own.

Assuming multiplying always makes things bigger

4.7 × 0.1 = 0.47. You have taken one tenth of 4.7, and a tenth of something is smaller than the thing. Multiplying only makes a number bigger when you multiply by something bigger than 1 — a fact worth saying out loud, because eight years of whole-number arithmetic taught you otherwise.

Assuming dividing always makes things smaller

8 ÷ 0.01 = 800. The question "how many hundredths fit into 8?" obviously has a big answer — there are a hundred of them in every 1. Dividing makes a number smaller only when you divide by something bigger than 1. This is the single most counter-intuitive line on the page, and it is worth two marks on every paper.

Confusing decimal places with significant figures

0.04728 to 2 decimal places is 0.05. To 2 significant figures it is 0.047. Decimal places count from the point; significant figures count from the first digit that isn't a leading zero. They give different answers and the question always says which it wants — read it twice.

Rounding twice

5.749 to 1 decimal place is 5.7, because the digit after the 7 is a 4. Rounding to 5.75 first and then to 5.8 is wrong — you have let a 9 push a 4 that had no business moving. Always round the original number in one step, and look only at the single digit immediately after the place you are keeping.
