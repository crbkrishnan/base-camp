What We Take, What We Leave

Almost every item on this syllabus is one of two things: we removed something an ecosystem was relying on, or we added something it cannot process. And the damage is almost always indirect — nobody poisons the fish, they feed the algae. Follow the chain and the marks follow you.

Five traps

Read before any test

“The greenhouse effect is bad and should be stopped”

The greenhouse effect is natural and necessary — without it the Earth would average about −18 °C. The problem is the enhanced greenhouse effect: extra carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide trapping more heat than before. Use the word “enhanced” and the mark is safe.

“The fertiliser poisons the fish”

Nothing in eutrophication is a poison. Nitrate feeds algae → algae block light → plants die → decomposer bacteria multiply → their aerobic respiration uses up the dissolved oxygen → fish suffocate. Six links, and the two most often dropped are the word light and the word aerobic.

“Deforestation is bad because trees give us oxygen”

True but weak, and rarely the mark being looked for. The rewarded answers are: less carbon dioxide removed by photosynthesis, carbon released from the felled wood, habitats destroyed, soil eroded once the roots have gone, and less transpiration so less local rainfall.

“Global warming just means everywhere gets slightly warmer”

A couple of degrees on a mean hides everything that matters. Sea level rises as ice melts and seawater expands; rainfall belts move, so some regions flood and others dry out; extreme events become more frequent; and species shift towards the poles and up mountains, with the ones that cannot move fast enough dying out.

Confusing the enhanced greenhouse effect, ozone depletion and acid rain

Three different problems, three different causes. Enhanced greenhouse effect: carbon dioxide and methane trapping heat. Ozone depletion: CFCs destroying the ozone layer, which lets more ultraviolet through. Acid rain: sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from burning fuels dissolving in rain, killing trees and acidifying lakes. Mentioning the ozone hole in a global-warming answer costs you the mark you were about to earn.
