Particles
That Push Back

Air feels like nothing. It is actually about ten thousand million million million particles per litre, every one of them moving, and a great many of them hitting you every second. Gas pressure is nothing more than the sum of those hits. Almost every mark in this topic is awarded for saying that carefully — and lost for saying it loosely.

The deal. You've been landing around 10 out of 15. This page is out of 30, so the honest target is 21 on the first pass and 26 on the second. On this topic the marks are in the sentences, not the sums. One phrase does most of the work — more collisions per unit area per second — and it is worth learning as a single unit, the way you learnt a formula.

The gas box

Thirty particles, a wall you can slide inwards and a temperature dial. The machine is not faking the reading: it counts every collision the particles actually make with the walls over a rolling two-and-a-half-second window and builds the pressure from that count. Squash the box and watch the collision counter move before the pressure does. The little magenta ticks are the individual hits.

The diffusion tube

A sixty-centimetre glass tube with a cotton wool plug at each end: ammonia on the left, hydrogen chloride on the right. Nothing stirs them and nothing blows them along. They spread by random motion alone, and where they meet a white ring of ammonium chloride forms. The ring does not appear in the middle. Run it and see where it does appear, then work out why.

Five traps

Read before any test

“The particles push harder because they are squashed”

This is the most expensive sentence in the topic, and it is wrong in a specific way: how hard a particle hits depends only on how fast it is moving, and squashing a gas does not speed anything up. What changes is how often a particle reaches a wall. Write more collisions per unit area per second and the mark is yours. Note that heating does make them hit harder — that is why the two situations need different sentences.

“The particles expand when heated”

They do not. A nitrogen molecule is exactly the same molecule at −50 °C and at 500 °C. The gas expands, if it is free to; the particles only move faster and end up further apart. Examiners are told to award nothing to any answer containing this idea, however good the rest of it is.

“You suck the drink up the straw”

Sucking describes your mouth, not the physics. You lower the pressure inside the straw, and the atmosphere pressing on the surface of the drink pushes the liquid up into that low-pressure region. Same for the crushed can: the vacuum does not pull it in, the atmosphere pushes it in. A low pressure never pulls. A high pressure pushes into it.

“Diffusion needs a draught, or stirring”

If something is being blown or stirred, that is convection or mixing, and the question was not about it. Diffusion is the net movement of particles caused by their own random motion, in perfectly still air. Answering “turn on a fan” to a question asking how to speed up diffusion replaces the process with a different one and scores nothing.

Thinking gas pressure only acts downwards

Water in a bucket pushes hardest on the bottom, because of its weight. A gas does not behave like that. Its particles move randomly in every direction, so the lid, the base and every side receive the same number of collisions per unit area per second and feel the same pressure. If a diagram shows arrows on only one wall, the diagram is wrong.
