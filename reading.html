Between the Lines

About seven of the twenty-five marks on your reading paper are for writing down what the text says. The other eighteen are for what it shows and what that means — and those are the ones you are leaving on the table. This page is one ladder, five tells and one sentence shape. That is the whole trick, and it works on any passage you are ever given.

The deal. Last topic you banked 10 out of 15. This page is out of 30, and the honest target is 21 on the first pass and 26 on the second. Marks only count if you write your answer before opening the mark scheme — otherwise you're just reading, and reading feels like learning without being learning.

Quick checks

Q1–Q5 · 5 marks

One try each, and the feedback tells you why. Q5 is the one that catches people who have just learned to infer: one of those four options is an invention, not an inference.

Short answers

Q6–Q10 · 10 marks

Write your answer first, then open the mark scheme and mark yourself honestly. Two marks means two separate ideas — one idea written twice in different words is one mark, every time.

Long answers

Q11–Q15 · 15 marks

Stuck? Take one hint, not the whole solution. Hint 1 gives you the line range, hint 2 names the tell, hint 3 gives you the word — none of them gives you the explanation, because that is the part worth marks. Q14 and Q15 are the two that separate a 21 from a 26.

Five traps

Read before any test

Copying three lines and calling it a quotation

A long quotation is a confession that you have not found the part that matters. The examiner is looking for evidence that you can locate the words doing the work — if you quote the whole sentence, you have shown the opposite. Quote the shortest run of words that still carries the meaning, then zoom to one word inside it. Five words beats fifty.

“This shows he is scared.”

True, and worth about half of what you think. You have named a feeling but not shown how the words produce it, so there is nothing for the second mark to land on. Add the zoom: which word, and what that word is doing. “‘Shakes it again’ — the repeat shows he knows it is hopeless and cannot make himself stop.”

Answering from outside the line range

When a question says look at lines 14–19, an answer from line 31 scores zero even when it is the better answer. This is the cheapest mark on the whole paper to lose, and it is lost by not re-reading the instruction. Put your finger on the first and last line named before you start hunting.

Two quotations that make the same point

Four-mark questions ask for two quotations and two explanations. If your second quotation proves what the first already proved, the second explanation earns nothing — you have written one answer twice. Before you commit to the second, check it is a different kind of evidence: a body, a repeat, a word choice, a gap, a turn.

Inventing instead of inferring

“She is lonely because her husband has died” is not reading between the lines, it is writing your own story in the gap. Before any inference, find the word you would underline if the examiner challenged you. No word, no mark — an overreach scores exactly the same as a blank, and it costs you more time.
