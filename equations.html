Both Sides

An equation says two things weigh exactly the same. Everything you are allowed to do follows from that one sentence: whatever you do to one pan, you must do to the other, or the claim stops being true. Solving is not a set of tricks. It is taking the scales apart without ever tipping them.

The deal. You've been landing around 10 out of 15. This page is out of 30, so the honest target is 21 on the first pass and 26 on the second. Every single answer on this page can be checked by putting it back into the original equation. If you do that every time, you should never hand in a wrong answer again — you will only ever hand in an unfinished one.

The balance scales

Each pan holds the two sides of the equation. The beam is level because the equation is true — and it stays level only while both pans are treated identically. Take something off one pan alone and watch it tip. That tip is the whole reason for the rule.

Five traps

Read before any test

Doing it to one side only

Taking 4 off the left and leaving the right alone turns a true statement into a false one. Once the beam has tipped, every line after it is wrong, however neat the working looks. Write the operation down the side of your page — − 4 — so you can see at a glance that it happened twice.

Moving a term across without changing its sign

From 3x + 4 = 19, the 4 becomes −4 on the other side, giving 3x = 15. Writing 3x = 23 means you added it instead of subtracting. If you find "moving across" slippery, don't move anything — just subtract 4 from both sides and let the 4 disappear on its own.

Dividing only part of a side

3x + 6 = 18 does not become x + 6 = 6. Dividing by 3 divides everything on both sides, giving x + 2 = 6. This is why you strip the numbers off first and divide last — then there is only ever one term left to divide.

Not expanding the bracket before you start

4(x + 3) = 20 is not 4x + 3 = 20. Either expand properly to 4x + 12 = 20, or divide both sides by 4 first to get x + 3 = 5. Both give x = 2. What you cannot do is half-expand and carry on.

Not checking, when checking is free

Every answer here can be tested in ten seconds by putting it back into the original equation — not into your own second line, which may already be wrong. If both sides come out the same number, you are right. There is no other topic in mathematics where the marking scheme is this available to you, and skipping it is simply choosing to lose marks.
