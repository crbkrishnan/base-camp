Disappearing Act

Stir sugar into tea and it vanishes. It has not gone anywhere: every particle is still in the cup, now spread out between the water particles, and the cup weighs exactly what the tea and the sugar weighed separately. This page is about what "dissolve" really means, how much a liquid can hold, and what changes that number — as against what only changes how fast it happens.

The deal. You've been landing around 10 out of 15. This page is out of 30, so the honest target is 21 on the first pass and 26 on the second. Two things pay here: the four words solute, solvent, solution, saturated used exactly, and knowing that stirring changes the speed of dissolving but not the amount.

Five traps

Read before any test

Saying the solute "disappears" or "melts"

It does neither. Dissolving is the solute breaking up into particles too small to see and spreading out between the solvent particles. Melting is a solid turning to liquid on its own with heat. Sugar in cold water dissolves without melting; a lump of butter in a hot pan melts without dissolving. Write "dissolves" and "spreads out between the water particles".

Thinking stirring makes more dissolve

Stirring, crushing and (mostly) warming make the solute dissolve faster. Only temperature changes how much can dissolve. If a solution is saturated, stir it all day and the pile on the bottom stays the same size. The question "how could you dissolve more?" has one answer — heat it — and "how could you dissolve it faster?" has three.

Leaving the temperature off a solubility

"The solubility of potassium nitrate is 32 g per 100 g of water" is incomplete — at 60 °C it is 110 g. A solubility without a temperature is not a number an examiner can mark. Same for "saturated": a solution saturated at 60 °C is nowhere near saturated at 20 °C, which is exactly why crystals fall out when it cools.

Assuming everything gets more soluble when heated

Most solids do. Sodium chloride barely does — 36 g at 20 °C, 40 g at 100 °C. And gases go the other way: warm water holds less dissolved oxygen and less dissolved carbon dioxide, which is why a warm fizzy drink goes flat faster and why fish struggle in a warm pond. Say which kind of substance you are talking about.

Believing the mass drops when something dissolves

50 g of water plus 10 g of salt is 60 g of salt solution, before and after, stirred or not. Nothing left the beaker. If the reading on a balance falls, some water evaporated — say so. Conservation of mass is tested in this topic every year, usually by a question that looks like it is about something else.
