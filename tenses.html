Tenses and Pronouns

काल और सर्वनाम

Every Hindi verb wears two name tags. The ending says whether the doer is a boy or a girl, one or many. A small helper at the back says who: मैं, तुम, आप, वह. Pronouns play the same trick from the other side — मैं turns into मुझे or मेरा depending on what gets stuck next to it. Learn which tag copies what, and six tenses, three levels of “please” and eight pronouns collapse into tables you can build in your head.

The deal. Last topic you banked 10 out of 15. This page is out of 30, and the honest target is 21 on the first pass and 26 on the second. Marks only count if you write your answer before opening the mark scheme. One thing is different on this page: every time you speak or write about yourself, you are a girl, so the verb wears the feminine tag — मैं जाती हूँ, never जाता. Most marks lost on this topic are that one ending.

Five traps

Read before any test

“मैं जाता हूँ” — said by a girl

The ending copies the doer, and the doer is you. The instinct is to copy the pattern from the textbook, and the textbook is usually written from a boy's point of view. मैं स्कूल जाती हूँ, मैं खा रही थी, मैं गई थी, मैं आऊँगी. The helper (हूँ) stays the same; the ending is the tag that flips. It is the single easiest mark to lose on this whole page, because the wrong version looks perfectly normal.

The helper does not match the person

मैं जाती है, वह जाती हूँ, तुम जाते है. Two tags, two jobs: the ending matches gender and number, the helper matches the person. And तुम and आप are always treated as plural by the verb, even when you mean one person: तुम जाते हो / जाती हो, आप जाती हैं — never तुम जाता हो.

ने everywhere, or ने nowhere

मैंने स्कूल गई and मैंने खा रही हूँ are both wrong. ने belongs to the “done” forms of verbs that take an object (मैंने रोटी खाई, मैंने रोटी खाई है) and to nothing else — not जाना, आना, सोना, not the continuous, not the future, not चुका. And when it does appear, the verb copies the object, so मैंने रोटी खाई but मैंने आम खाया — your own gender is not in the verb at all.

“मैं को”, “मैं के साथ”

A pronoun never keeps its plain shape in front of a marker. मैं + को = मुझे, मैं + से = मुझसे, वह + ने = उसने, वे + ने = उन्होंने. And के लिए / के साथ do not take the oblique base at all; they take the possessive: मेरे लिए, तुम्हारे साथ, उसके लिए. The English word “for me” has no shape-change to warn you.

“his” is not always उसका

राधा उसकी किताब पढ़ती है means Radha is reading someone else's book. If it is her own, the word is अपनी. The test: does the possessor equal the subject of the sentence? Yes → अपना / अपनी / अपने (which copy the thing owned, not the owner). No → उसका / उनका. English uses one word, “her”, for both, which is exactly why you will write the wrong one.
