The sliding apex

Triangle ABC sits on base BC. The top corner A rides along a dashed line parallel to the base. Slide A as far as you like — the triangle leans, stretches, goes obtuse — and its area doesn't change, because the base and the height don't.

Five traps

Read before any test

Using a slanted side as the height

The height is the perpendicular distance from the apex to the base line. In an obtuse triangle it lands outside the triangle — slide the apex past B on the tool and watch. Using the side AB instead of the height gives a bigger, wrong area every time.

Using Pythagoras on the wrong side

c² = a² + b² only when c is the hypotenuse — the longest side, opposite the right angle. If you're given a leg and the hypotenuse, you subtract: a² = c² − b². And it only works for right-angled triangles at all.

Scaling area by k instead of k²

If two similar triangles have sides in ratio 1 : 3, their areas are in ratio 1 : 9. Lengths scale by k, areas by k², volumes by k³. A photo enlarged to twice the width uses four times the paper.

Matching the wrong sides in similar triangles

Corresponding sides face equal angles. Write the triangles with their equal angles in the same order (ADE ~ ABC) and read the ratios straight off: AD/AB = AE/AC = DE/BC. Matching by 'the one that looks the same size' goes wrong whenever the small triangle is flipped.

Thinking √(a² + b²) = a + b

√(9 + 16) = 5, not 3 + 4 = 7. Square roots don't split over addition. The hypotenuse is always shorter than the two legs added together — it's the straight route.
