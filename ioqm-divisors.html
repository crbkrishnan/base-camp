The divisor grid

Build a number out of 2s, 3s and 5s. Every divisor is one choice of how many 2s, how many 3s and how many 5s to keep — so the divisors fall into a grid, and counting them is just counting the cells. Ochre cells are the perfect squares.

Five traps

Read before any test

Gluing two rules together when the numbers share a factor

Divisible by 4 and by 6 does not mean divisible by 24 — 12 is divisible by both and isn't. You can only combine rules for numbers with no common factor: 24 = 3 × 8, so test 3 and 8. Likewise 12 = 3 × 4 and 36 = 4 × 9, never 2 × 6 or 3 × 12.

Reading the alternating sum wrongly for 11

Start from the units digit with a plus, then alternate. A result of 0 counts — 0 is a multiple of 11 — and so do 11, −11 and 22. Students who get 0 often write "not divisible" because nothing is left. It is divisible.

Forgetting the +1 when counting divisors

72 = 2³ × 3² has (3+1)(2+1) = 12 divisors, not 3 × 2 = 6. The +1 is the choice of taking none of that prime. Leave it out and you have forgotten that 1, 8 and 9 are divisors of 72.

Using the digit-sum test for 27

The digit sum decides 3 and 9 and nothing else. 99 has digit sum 18, which is a multiple of 27's friend 9 — but 99 ÷ 27 is not whole. The test works because 10 leaves remainder 1 on division by 9; it leaves remainder 10 on division by 27, so the trick dies.

Reporting 0 as the HCF

In the Euclidean algorithm you stop when a remainder hits 0, and the answer is the last non-zero remainder. If your final line is 34 = 2 × 17 + 0, the HCF is 17. Writing 0 throws away the whole calculation in the last second.
